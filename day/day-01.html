1. Introduction — Why Financial Services Exist

  • Money is a medium of exchange, a measure of wealth, and a store of value
  • A financial intermediary channels funds from those in surplus (savers) to those in deficit (borrowers)
  • Intermediation: money flows through a firm, e.g., a bank takes deposits and makes loans
  • Disintermediation: savers lend directly to borrowers without an intermediary — e.g., peer-to-peer (P2P) lending
Exam Trap: P2P lending is not covered by the FSCS. Investors bear full credit risk and can lose their entire investment.

2. The Bank of England (BoE)

Founded 1694. The UK's central bank. It has five statutory roles:

  1. Monetary Policy — the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) sets Bank Rate to keep CPI inflation at 2%
  2. Lender of Last Resort — provides emergency liquidity to banks that are solvent but temporarily illiquid
  3. Financial Stability — the Financial Policy Committee (FPC) monitors and reduces systemic risk across the whole system
  4. Resolution — the BoE manages the orderly wind-down of a failing bank to minimise economic harm
  5. Issuing banknotes — the sole legal issuer of notes in England and Wales (Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own note-issuing banks)
Regulator distinction: The BoE does not regulate financial advisers — the FCA does. The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), a part of the BoE, supervises banks, building societies, credit unions, insurers, and major investment firms.

3. Proprietary vs Mutual Organisations

Proprietary
  • Owned by shareholders
  • Profit motive — returns go to shareholders
  • Typically a PLC listed on a stock exchange
  • Examples: Barclays plc, HSBC Holdings plc
Mutual
  • Owned by members (its depositors and/or borrowers)
  • Surplus profits returned to members as better rates or lower charges
  • More conservative lending culture — no shareholder dividend pressure
  • Examples: Nationwide BS, friendly societies
  • Can demutualise — members receive shares on conversion (e.g., Halifax → HBOS)
Key rule: Building societies are always mutuals. If they demutualise they become proprietary (shareholder-owned) banks.

4. Credit Unions

  • Member-owned co-operative financial institutions — distinct from building societies
  • Membership requires a common bond: members must share the same employer, live/work in the same geographic area, or belong to the same association
  • Offer savings accounts and loans only to qualifying members
  • Regulated by the FCA
  • Deposits protected by FSCS up to £85,000 per member
  • Loan interest is capped by statute at 3% per month (equivalent to 42.6% APR)
Exam Trap: Credit unions require a common bond — anyone can join a building society. Do not confuse the two.

5. Retail vs Wholesale Banking

Retail Banking
  • Services provided to individuals and SMEs
  • Current accounts, savings, mortgages, personal loans, credit cards
  • Large volume of small transactions
  • Examples: HSBC, Barclays, Nationwide
Wholesale Banking
  • Services between financial institutions and large corporates
  • Interbank lending, bond issuance, syndicated loans, trade finance
  • Small number of very large transactions
  • Examples: investment banking divisions, interbank market

6. How Lenders Fund Their Lending

  • Retail deposits — money from individual savers; most stable, but more expensive to administer
  • Wholesale funding — borrowing from other banks or capital markets; cheaper to access but highly volatile (can disappear overnight in a crisis)
  • Securitisation — the lender pools mortgage loans into securities (MBS) and sells them to investors, freeing up capital to lend again
  • Covered bonds — secured debt backed by a ring-fenced pool of mortgages; lower cost of funds than unsecured bonds
2008 lesson — Northern Rock: Northern Rock funded ~75% of its lending through wholesale markets. When global credit markets froze in August 2007, its funding collapsed — triggering the first UK bank run since 1866. Retail deposits are far more stable.

7. The Role of the European Union

  • Until Brexit (31 January 2020), EU directives were directly implemented into UK law — passporting rights allowed firms to operate across the EU
  • Key EU directives that shaped UK financial law: MiFID II (investment services), CRD IV (bank capital adequacy), PSD2 (payment services), GDPR (data protection), IMD (insurance mediation)
  • Post-Brexit: EU law no longer applies directly; the UK retains its own adapted versions under domestic legislation
  • The cornerstone of UK financial regulation remains the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA)
  • Passporting into the EU is no longer available to UK firms — firms need separate EU authorisation

Practice Questions

Q1. Which body within the Bank of England is responsible for setting interest rates to meet the 2% CPI inflation target?
A. Financial Policy Committee (FPC)
B. Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA)
C. Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)
D. Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)
Reveal answer
Answer: C — Monetary Policy Committee (MPC). The MPC sets the Bank Rate targeting 2% CPI. The FPC handles systemic financial stability. The PRA and FCA are regulators, not BoE committees.
Q2. A saver deposits £95,000 into a peer-to-peer (P2P) lending platform. The platform subsequently fails. How much is protected by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS)?
A. £85,000
B. £50,000
C. The full £95,000
D. Nothing — P2P lending is not FSCS protected
Reveal answer
Answer: D. P2P lending platforms are not authorised deposit-takers and are explicitly excluded from FSCS deposit protection. The saver could lose the entire £95,000.
Q3. Which of the following best describes a mutual building society?
A. A company owned by shareholders seeking dividend returns
B. A co-operative that requires members to share a common bond
C. An organisation owned by its depositors and borrowers with no external shareholders
D. A government-backed institution regulated by HM Treasury
Reveal answer
Answer: C. Building societies are mutuals — owned by members (depositors and borrowers). There are no external shareholders. Common bond is a credit union requirement, not a building society requirement.
