Financial products underpinning savings, investment, and mortgage advice. Understanding risk/return trade-offs and tax treatment is essential for client suitability.

1. Deposits

Bank and building society deposits are the most common savings vehicle in the UK. They are simple, accessible, and protected by the FSCS up to £85,000 per person per authorised firm (£170,000 for joint accounts).

Account typeKey features
Instant accessWithdrawals on demand; lowest interest rate
Notice accountTypically 30/60/90 days notice to withdraw; higher rate
Fixed-rate bondFixed term (1–5 yr); higher rate; early access usually penalised
Regular savingsCommit to monthly deposits; promotional rate for 12 months
Current accountDay-to-day transactions; low or zero interest
Exam Trap: The FSCS protects £85,000 per authorised firm, not per account. If a client holds £100,000 in two accounts at the same bank (same authorisation), only £85,000 is protected. Two separate banks = £85,000 each.

2. National Savings and Investments (NS&I)

NS&I is a government-backed savings institution. All products are 100% secure — there is no FSCS limit because the UK government guarantees the full amount.

ProductKey facts
Premium BondsMin £25, max £50,000. Prizes instead of interest. Tax-free prizes. Prize fund rate varies. No interest earned.
Income BondsVariable rate, monthly interest paid to bank account. Min £500, max £1,000,000.
Direct SaverVariable rate, online only. Min £1, max £2,000,000.
Fixed Interest Savings CertificatesFixed rate, fixed term (1 or 2 year). Tax-free returns. Currently on sale periodically.
Index-Linked Savings CertificatesReturn linked to CPI + fixed %. Tax-free. Currently not on general sale.
Green Savings Bonds3-year fixed; funds UK government green projects; tax-free interest.
NS&I Direct ISACash ISA; variable rate; counts toward annual ISA allowance.
Key point: NS&I products are backed by HM Treasury. The savings limit on Premium Bonds is £50,000 per person. Prize payments are completely tax-free.

3. Offshore Deposits

Deposits held in jurisdictions outside the UK — most commonly the Channel Islands or Isle of Man.

  • Potential advantages: may offer higher interest rates; useful for internationally mobile clients
  • NOT covered by UK FSCS: protected only by the local compensation scheme (Channel Islands/IOM schemes offer limited cover)
  • Tax: UK residents must declare all offshore interest to HMRC — offshore â  tax-free. Interest accrues gross but must be declared on self-assessment
  • Currency risk: if account is in a foreign currency
  • Uses: legitimate tax deferral (not avoidance) where interest rolls up offshore and is declared when remitted; useful for non-domiciled individuals using remittance basis
Exam Trap: Offshore deposits are NOT tax-exempt for UK residents. The interest is taxable when received or credited. Do not confuse 'offshore' with 'tax-free'.

4. Money Market Instruments

Short-term debt instruments used by banks, governments, and large corporates to manage short-term funding. Typically mature within one year.

Treasury Bills (T-Bills)
  • Issued by UK government via DMO
  • Typically 91-day term
  • Issued at a discount to face value; no coupon
  • Return = difference between issue price and £100 face value
Certificates of Deposit (CDs)
  • Issued by banks acknowledging a deposit
  • Fixed term, fixed interest rate
  • Tradeable in secondary market — provides liquidity
Commercial Paper
  • Short-term unsecured corporate debt
  • Issued by large creditworthy companies
  • Issued at a discount; typically <270 days
Repos
  • Sale and repurchase agreement
  • Sell asset + agree to buy back at higher price later
  • Effectively a secured short-term loan
  • Used by BoE to implement monetary policy

5. Fixed Interest Securities

Bonds that pay a fixed coupon (regular interest payment) until the redemption date when the face value is repaid. Understanding yield is essential.

ConceptExplanation
Par value (nominal)Face value — typically £100. Bond redeemed at par unless otherwise stated.
CouponAnnual interest as a % of par (e.g., a '5% Treasury Gilt 2030' pays £5/year per £100 nominal)
Flat yield (running yield)Coupon Ã· market price Ã 100. Ignores capital gain/loss at redemption.
Redemption yield (yield to maturity)Total return including coupon AND capital gain/loss to redemption. The most complete measure.
Yield curvePlot of yields for different maturities. Normally upward sloping (longer = higher yield = more risk).
Critical Exam Point — Inverse Relationship: When market interest rates rise, existing bond prices FALL (and vice versa). If you hold a 3% bond and new bonds offer 5%, your bond is less attractive → its price falls. This is the most-tested fixed interest concept.

6. Government Gilts

UK government bonds issued by HM Treasury via the Debt Management Office (DMO). 'Gilt-edged' = very low default risk (sovereign risk only).

Conventional Gilts
  • Fixed coupon paid half-yearly
  • Redeemed at par on maturity date
  • Named by coupon & year: e.g., 4% Treasury Gilt 2032
  • Safest fixed interest investment
Index-Linked Gilts
  • Coupon AND redemption value linked to RPI
  • Protect against inflation (real return)
  • Real yield = nominal yield â inflation
  • Lower coupon than conventional gilts

Why gilts matter for mortgages: Swap rates (which determine fixed mortgage rates) are closely tied to gilt yields. When gilt yields rise, fixed mortgage rates tend to rise.

7. Corporate Bonds

Debt securities issued by companies to raise capital. Higher yield than gilts to compensate for credit risk.

  • Credit ratings: issued by S&P, Moody's, Fitch. Investment grade: BBBâ/Baa3 and above. Below = high-yield ('junk') bonds.
  • Debenture: corporate bond secured on the company's assets — lower risk than unsecured bonds
  • Subordinated bonds: lower ranking in insolvency — higher yield compensates for greater risk
  • Spread: extra yield over gilts = credit spread; reflects perceived default risk
Risk vs return: AAA corporate bond → lowest yield. CCC high-yield bond → highest yield but highest default risk. Gilts = benchmark (risk-free rate).

8. Local Authority Bonds

Bonds issued by UK local authorities (councils) to fund capital expenditure.

  • Slightly higher yield than equivalent gilts (local authorities cannot print money)
  • Considered very safe — backed by the local authority's power to levy council tax
  • Less liquid than gilts (smaller market)
  • Fixed term, fixed rate
  • Not covered by FSCS in the same way as bank deposits
  • Also issued via the Public Works Loan Board (PWLB) — local authorities borrow directly from central government

Practice Questions

Q1. A client holds £120,000 in two current accounts at the same authorised bank — £60,000 in each account. How much is protected by the FSCS?
A. £120,000 — both accounts are covered separately
B. £85,000 — the FSCS limit applies per authorised firm, not per account
C. £60,000 — only the first account is covered
D. £170,000 — joint account rules apply
Reveal answer
Answer: B — £85,000. The FSCS limit of £85,000 applies per person per authorised firm. Even though the money is in two separate accounts, both are at the same firm. The £170,000 limit applies to joint accounts (shared between two people), not two separate single accounts at the same bank.
Q2. Market interest rates rise from 3% to 5%. What happens to the market price of an existing 3% conventional gilt?
A. It rises, because higher rates make all bonds more valuable
B. It remains unchanged — gilts are backed by the government
C. It falls, because newer bonds offer a higher return, making the existing gilt less attractive
D. It falls only if the gilt is index-linked
Reveal answer
Answer: C. This is the fundamental inverse price/yield relationship. When new bonds are issued at 5%, an existing bond paying only 3% must trade at a lower price so that its yield is competitive. Price and yield move in opposite directions. This is a core exam concept.
Q3. Which NS&I product pays prizes instead of interest, and what is the maximum investment allowed per person?
A. NS&I Income Bonds; maximum £1,000,000
B. NS&I Premium Bonds; maximum £50,000
C. NS&I Premium Bonds; maximum £25,000
D. NS&I Direct Saver; maximum £2,000,000
Reveal answer
Answer: B — NS&I Premium Bonds; maximum £50,000. Premium Bonds pay no interest. Instead, all potential interest is pooled into a prize fund and distributed as tax-free cash prizes. The minimum holding is £25 and the maximum is £50,000 per person. All NS&I products are 100% secure (no FSCS limit applies).
