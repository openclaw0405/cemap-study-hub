42. Business Protection Insurance

Business protection covers the financial impact on a business when a key person, shareholder, or partner dies or becomes seriously ill.

Shareholder / Partnership Protection

  • Each shareholder/partner insures the others' lives (and often CI)
  • On death, the policy pays out to enable surviving shareholders to buy the deceased's share from their estate
  • Without this: the estate may force a sale to an unwanted third party, or surviving shareholders cannot afford to buy out the share — business paralysis
Cross-option agreement
  • Gives surviving shareholders the option to buy the deceased's shares
  • Gives the deceased's personal representatives (estate) the option to sell
  • Neither party is forced — both have an option
  • IHT advantage: Business Property Relief (BPR) is preserved as there is no binding obligation to sell at death
Business Loan Protection
  • Covers outstanding business loans (director's personal guarantee)
  • Pays off the loan if the director/key person dies or suffers CI
  • Prevents lender calling in a loan (and potentially triggering insolvency) on death
  • Life cover = loan amount; term = loan term
Exam Trap: A cross-option agreement (double option) preserves Business Property Relief for IHT because there is no legal obligation to sell at death. A buy-sell agreement (compulsory purchase) removes BPR because there IS a binding obligation — the shares lose their business property status. Always recommend cross-option, not buy-sell, where BPR preservation matters.

43. Key Person Insurance

The company takes out a life (and/or CI) policy on an employee whose death or serious illness would cause significant financial loss to the business. The company is both policyholder and beneficiary.

Purpose
  • Compensate for lost profits caused by key person's absence
  • Cover costs of finding/training a replacement
  • Reassure banks and investors that the business can continue
  • Cover during a business loan guarantee period
Tax treatment (HMRC practice)
  • Premiums are tax-deductible as a trading expense if ALL four conditions are met:
    1. Policy is term assurance (not whole of life / investment element)
    2. Policy has no surrender value
    3. Cover is for loss of profits, not capital
    4. The key person is an employee or director
  • If premiums are deductible: proceeds are taxable as trading receipts
  • If premiums are NOT deductible: proceeds are NOT taxable
Exam Trap: Tax treatment of key person insurance is symmetrical — if premiums are tax-deductible, the proceeds are taxable. If premiums are NOT deductible (e.g., whole of life policy with surrender value), the proceeds are NOT taxed. Many students get this backwards. Always link deductibility of premiums to taxability of proceeds.

44. General Insurance — Principles

Six core principles govern all general insurance contracts:

PrincipleExplanationExample
IndemnityInsurer restores insured to same financial position as before — no profit from a claimCar worth £8,000 written off → insurer pays £8,000, not £10,000
Insurable interestInsured must have a financial interest in the subject matter at the time of loss (property: also at inception)Landlord can insure own property; cannot insure neighbour's property
Utmost good faith (Uberrimae Fidei)Both parties must disclose all material facts — Consumer Insurance Act 2012 requires consumers to take reasonable care not to misrepresentMust disclose previous claims, criminal convictions, property modifications
SubrogationAfter paying a claim, insurer steps into insured's shoes and can pursue recovery from a third partyCar damaged by negligent driver; insurer pays, then sues the driver
ContributionWhere same risk is covered by two policies, each insurer pays proportionate share; insured cannot recover more than the lossTwo contents policies covering same property — each pays half
Proximate causeThe dominant, effective cause of the loss (not the nearest in time) — must be an insured perilStorm causes tree to fall on house: proximate cause = storm (insured); not the weight of the tree
Average clause: If property is underinsured, the insurer applies the average clause — claim is reduced proportionally. E.g., property insured for £100,000 but rebuild cost is £200,000 (50% underinsured) → insurer pays only 50% of any valid claim.

46. Mortgages

A mortgage is a loan secured by a legal charge on real property. The borrower (mortgagor) gives the lender (mortgagee) a legal interest in the property as security. If the borrower defaults, the lender can repossess and sell to recover the debt.

Regulatory framework
  • Regulated by FCA under MCOB (Mortgage Conduct of Business Rules)
  • First charge mortgages on residential property: regulated
  • Buy-to-let: generally unregulated (except consumer BTL — accidental landlords)
  • Consumer BTL: regulated since March 2016 (EU Mortgage Credit Directive)
Charges
  • First charge: primary mortgage; has priority in repossession
  • Second charge: additional secured loan; junior to first charge; must be repaid after first charge on sale
  • Land Registry: legal charge must be registered to be enforceable
  • SDLT: payable by buyer on purchase; additional 3% surcharge for second homes/BTL

47. Types of Mortgage

Repayment (Capital and Interest)
  • Each monthly payment covers both interest charged and a portion of the outstanding capital
  • In early years: mostly interest, little capital repaid
  • Guaranteed to be repaid in full at end of term if all payments made
  • Most common type; recommended by FCA as default for residential mortgages
Interest-Only
  • Monthly payment covers interest only — capital balance unchanged throughout term
  • Capital must be repaid in full at end of term via a separate repayment vehicle
  • Lower monthly payment but total interest paid is higher over the term
  • FCA requires lenders to assess plausibility of repayment strategy
  • Common in BTL; declining in residential use
Part-and-Part: Some borrowers split their mortgage — part on repayment, part on interest-only. Provides flexibility while ensuring some capital reduction over time. Each portion needs separate assessment.

48. Mortgage Payment Vehicles

Interest-only borrowers must have a credible strategy to repay the capital at the end of the mortgage term. The FCA requires this to be assessed.

VehicleHow it worksKey risk
Endowment policyRegular premiums build a pot; pays out at maturity to repay mortgageNo guarantee; many 1990s policies suffered shortfalls; rarely used now
ISAAnnual contributions invested in Stocks & Shares ISA; tax-free growth; aim to accumulate capital by term endInvestment risk; no guarantee pot will reach mortgage balance
Pension (PCLS)Take 25% tax-free cash lump sum at retirement to repay mortgage capitalMust retire before or at mortgage end date; pension fund must be sufficient; pension access rules may change
Sale of propertySell the property at end of term; common for BTL investorsProperty values could fall; suitable only if borrower has other residence
Investments (bonds, savings)General investment portfolio accumulated over termNo guarantee; taxation of gains outside ISA wrapper
Exam Trap: Endowment policies are no longer considered a reliable payment vehicle — many resulted in shortfalls. The FCA requires lenders to have a plausible repayment strategy for interest-only mortgages — "will sell the property" is only acceptable if the borrower has other accommodation. Using pension tax-free cash requires the retirement date to align with or precede the mortgage term end.

Early Repayment Charges (ERCs)
  • Penalty for leaving (remortgaging) or overpaying beyond agreed limits during initial deal period
  • Typically 1–5% of outstanding balance
  • Expressed as a % declining over the deal period (e.g., 5% in year 1, 4% in year 2...)
  • No ERCs on SVR
Offset mortgage mechanics
  • Mortgage £200,000 / Savings £50,000 → interest charged on £150,000 only
  • Savings earn no stated interest but the effective return = mortgage interest rate (often better than savings rates)
  • Savings remain accessible (unlike overpayments on standard mortgage)
  • Particularly tax-efficient for higher-rate taxpayers who would pay tax on savings interest

50. Releasing Equity for Elderly

Equity release allows older homeowners (typically 55+) to access value tied up in their property without selling. Two main types regulated by the FCA:

Lifetime Mortgage
  • A loan secured on the property; most common type of equity release
  • No monthly repayments required (typically)
  • Interest rolls up (compounds) — debt grows significantly over time
  • Repaid from proceeds when property is sold (on death or permanent entry into long-term care)
  • No Negative Equity Guarantee (NNEG): Equity Release Council members must ensure debt never exceeds property value — borrower / estate can never owe more than the property is worth
  • FCA regulated
  • Optional: drawdown facility rather than single lump sum
Home Reversion Plan
  • Homeowner sells part or all of the property to the reversion company at below market value
  • Retains the right to live in the property rent-free (or for nominal rent) for life
  • NOT a loan — no interest rolls up
  • No debt accumulation, but the sold share is gone permanently
  • The homeowner (estate) only benefits from a proportion of any future property price increase
  • FCA regulated
Equity Release Council (ERC): Trade body that sets standards for the equity release market. Members must: offer No Negative Equity Guarantee, allow borrowers to move to another suitable property (portability), allow interest to be serviced voluntarily, ensure independent legal advice is taken.
Exam Trap: Rolling up interest on a lifetime mortgage can dramatically erode the estate — at 6% interest, a debt doubles in approximately 12 years. The NNEG protects against owing more than the property value, but the estate may receive very little. Also: equity release can affect entitlement to means-tested benefits (e.g., Pension Credit) because cash received increases capital assets.

53. Self-Invested Personal Pension (SIPP)

A SIPP is a type of defined contribution personal pension that gives the member a much wider range of investment choices than a standard personal pension. It is FCA regulated.

Permitted investments (CAN invest in)
  • Stocks and shares (UK and international)
  • Funds: unit trusts, OEICs, investment trusts
  • ETFs
  • Government gilts and corporate bonds
  • Commercial property (offices, industrial units, retail — but NOT residential)
  • Cash deposits
  • Unlisted shares (small business investments)
Prohibited investments (CANNOT invest in)
  • Residential property (houses, flats for letting) — HMRC taxable property rules
  • Tangible moveable property: art, antiques, fine wine, classic cars, jewellery
  • Cryptocurrency (currently HMRC treats as speculative — many SIPP providers exclude)
  • Holiday property, residential land

Accessing SIPP benefits — flexible access from age 55 (rising to 57 in 2028)

Access methodDescriptionTax treatment
PCLS (Pension Commencement Lump Sum)25% tax-free cash at crystallisation; remaining 75% stays in drawdown or converts to annuity25% tax-free; 75% taxable when drawn
DrawdownFund remains invested; member draws income as needed; flexible amountsEach withdrawal: taxable as income at marginal rate (except 25% PCLS taken separately)
UFPLS (Uncrystallised Fund Pension Lump Sum)Ad hoc lump sums from uncrystallised fund; no separate PCLS taken25% of each payment tax-free; 75% taxable as income — EACH TIME
AnnuityExchange fund for guaranteed income for lifeAnnuity income: fully taxable
SSAS (Small Self-Administered Scheme): Occupational pension for small companies (often owner-managed). Unlike SIPPs, a SSAS can lend money back to the sponsoring employer (up to 50% of scheme assets on commercial terms). Can also invest in employer's commercial property. Trustee-based governance.
Exam Trap: SIPPs CANNOT invest in residential property — this is a HMRC taxable property rule; any attempt to do so triggers a 55% tax charge (unauthorised payment surcharge). Commercial property IS permitted. UFPLS means every payment is 25% tax-free / 75% taxable — different from taking PCLS upfront where the 25% is separated at crystallisation.

Exam Traps — Day 10

  • Key person tax: Deductible premiums = taxable proceeds. Non-deductible premiums = non-taxable proceeds. Symmetrical rule.
  • Cross-option vs buy-sell: Cross-option preserves Business Property Relief (BPR). Buy-sell agreement removes BPR — avoid recommending buy-sell for IHT-sensitive estates.
  • Employers' Liability: Compulsory statutory requirement — minimum £5m cover. Public liability is NOT compulsory by statute.
  • Rebuild cost â  market value: Buildings insurance must be based on rebuild cost; land value is irrelevant (can't be destroyed by fire).
  • Offset mortgage: Savings balance reduces interest charged — effective return = mortgage interest rate. Savings earn no explicit interest but are accessible.
  • Equity release NNEG: Equity Release Council members must offer No Negative Equity Guarantee. Debt can never exceed property value.
  • Lifetime Mortgage vs Home Reversion: Lifetime = loan (interest rolls up); Home Reversion = sale of share (no debt, below market value, rent-free occupancy).
  • MPAA: Once flexible drawdown is triggered, the MPAA of £10,000 applies to further DC contributions — not the full £60,000 AA.
  • SIPP residential property: Strictly prohibited; 55% unauthorised payment tax charge if attempted. Commercial property is allowed.
  • State Pension 2024/25: £221.20/week; needs 35 qualifying NI years; minimum 10 years for any State Pension.

Memorise

  • Term + no surrender value + covers profit loss = deductible premiums
  • Deductible premiums → proceeds TAXABLE
  • Non-deductible premiums → proceeds NOT taxable
Equity Release
  • Lifetime mortgage: loan, interest rolls up, NNEG, FCA regulated
  • Home reversion: sell share below market value, rent-free for life, no debt
  • Both: ERC standards, independent legal advice required
Pension Numbers 2024/25
  • Annual Allowance: £60,000
  • MPAA (after drawdown): £10,000
  • Tax-free cash: 25% of fund (max LSA £268,275)
  • State Pension: £221.20/week; 35 NI years
  • Auto-enrolment: 3% employer + 5% employee = 8%
SIPP investments
  • CAN: commercial property, shares, funds, bonds, ETFs
  • CANNOT: residential property, art/wine/cars/antiques
  • UFPLS: 25% tax-free + 75% taxable per payment
  • PCLS: 25% tax-free upfront; rest stays in fund

Practice Questions

Q1. ABC Ltd pays annual premiums on a 5-year term life assurance policy on its sales director. The policy has no surrender value and covers the company against lost profits if the director dies. The premiums are treated as tax-deductible. The director dies in year 3 and the insurer pays £500,000 to ABC Ltd. How should the proceeds be treated for tax purposes?
A. Tax-free, as life assurance proceeds are always exempt from tax
B. Taxable as a trading receipt, as the premiums were tax-deductible
C. Capital gains tax applies on the difference between premiums paid and proceeds received
D. 50% taxable, 50% exempt, as the policy covered both profit loss and capital
Reveal answer
Answer: B. HMRC's approach to key person insurance is symmetrical: if the premiums were allowed as a tax-deductible trading expense, the proceeds are treated as taxable trading receipts (income). Conversely, if the premiums were NOT deductible, the proceeds are NOT taxable. In this case, all four conditions for deductibility were met (term assurance, no surrender value, covers profit loss, employee/director), so the premiums were deductible — meaning the £500,000 proceeds are taxable as trading income. Life assurance proceeds are NOT automatically tax-free in a business context.
Q2. Miriam, aged 72, owns her home worth £400,000 with no outstanding mortgage. She takes out a lifetime mortgage for £80,000 at 5% per annum interest rolled up. Assuming no repayments are made, approximately how much will be owed after 14 years?
A. £80,000 — no repayments are required so the debt stays the same
B. £112,000 — simple interest of 5% per year adds £4,000 per year
C. £160,000 — the debt approximately doubles in 14 years due to compound roll-up
D. £320,000 — the debt quadruples as compound interest dramatically accelerates
Reveal answer
Answer: C. At 5% compound interest, a debt doubles approximately every 14 years (the Rule of 72: 72 Ã· 5 = 14.4 years). £80,000 Ã (1.05)^14 â £80,000 Ã 1.98 â £158,000 — effectively doubled. The NNEG is a mandatory requirement for Equity Release Council members.
Q3. David, aged 58, has a SIPP worth £400,000. He has never accessed his pension. He wants to take an ad hoc lump sum of £40,000 from the SIPP today without crystallising the whole fund. Which is the most appropriate method, and what is the tax treatment?
A. Pension Commencement Lump Sum (PCLS): the entire £40,000 is tax-free as it is within 25% of the fund
B. Uncrystallised Fund Pension Lump Sum (UFPLS): £10,000 tax-free and £30,000 taxable as income
C. Drawdown: David first crystallises the whole fund, takes 25% PCLS (£100,000), then draws £40,000 from the drawdown fund — fully taxable
D. UFPLS: the full £40,000 is tax-free as it represents less than 25% of the total fund value
Reveal answer
Answer: B. An Uncrystallised Fund Pension Lump Sum (UFPLS) allows David to take ad hoc lump sums directly from his uncrystallised (unaccessed) pension pot without crystallising the whole fund. Each UFPLS payment is treated as 25% tax-free and 75% taxable as income — regardless of size. So for a £40,000 UFPLS: £10,000 (25%) is tax-free and £30,000 (75%) is taxable as income at his marginal rate. Answer D is wrong — the 25% rule applies to EACH payment, not the percentage of total fund. Answer A is wrong — PCLS requires crystallisation of at least that tranche of the pension first. UFPLS is specifically designed for ad hoc access without full crystallisation.
