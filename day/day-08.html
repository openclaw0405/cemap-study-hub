25. Non-Mainstream Pooled Investments (NMPIs)

NMPIs are complex, unregulated or lightly regulated collective investment schemes not suitable for ordinary retail investors.

  • Examples: Unregulated Collective Investment Schemes (UCIs), traded life policy investments (TLPIs), some types of property funds, hedge funds, private equity funds
  • FCA restriction: NMPIs may only be promoted (marketed) to professional clients, sophisticated investors, or high-net-worth individuals
  • Key risks: illiquid, complex, opaque fee structures, no FSCS protection for investment losses, potentially high leverage
  • Standard retail clients cannot be recommended NMPIs through a standard financial promotion
Exam Trap: The NMPI marketing restriction means that advisers cannot simply recommend these products to any client. It is not just that they are unsuitable — there is a regulatory prohibition on promoting them to ordinary retail clients.

26. Investment Trusts

A closed-ended listed investment company. Unlike unit trusts/OEICs, the number of shares is fixed — you buy/sell existing shares on the stock exchange.

Gearing effect: If an investment trust borrows 25% of its assets to invest, and the portfolio rises 10%, the equity return is amplified. However if the portfolio falls, losses are amplified too. Gearing = magnification of both gains AND losses.

27. Open-Ended Investment Companies (OEICs)

Similar to unit trusts but in company rather than trust form. Open-ended: shares created/cancelled as investors buy/sell.

Key Features
  • Single price: one price for buying AND selling (unlike dual-priced unit trusts)
  • Authorised Corporate Director (ACD) manages the OEIC
  • Depositary safeguards assets (equivalent of trustee)
  • FCA-authorised; COLL rules apply
  • Swing pricing may be used to prevent dilution of existing investors by large flows
Umbrella Funds
  • Single OEIC can contain multiple sub-funds (e.g., UK equity, bond, global)
  • Investor can switch between sub-funds — may be CGT event
  • Each sub-fund has different charges and risk profiles
  • Simplifies administration under one legal structure

28. Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs) and Platforms

ETFs
  • Investment fund listed on a stock exchange — traded like a share throughout the day
  • Typically passive: tracks an index (e.g., FTSE 100, S&P 500)
  • Low costs — no active management fee; TER often 0.05%–0.3%
  • Diversified in one trade
  • Can be physical (holds actual assets) or synthetic (uses swaps/derivatives to replicate index)
  • Synthetic ETFs carry counterparty risk
  • Can be held in ISA or SIPP
Investment Platforms (Fund Supermarkets / Wrap Platforms)
  • Online services allowing investors to hold multiple products in one place
  • Examples: Hargreaves Lansdown, AJ Bell, Fidelity, Transact
  • Facilitate: fund selection, ISA/SIPP administration, portfolio reporting, switching between funds
  • Platform charges: may be a % of assets (e.g., 0.25%/year), flat fee, or per-transaction
  • Relevant for retail clients building a diversified portfolio efficiently

29. Individual Savings Accounts (ISAs)

Tax-efficient wrapper — income and gains within an ISA are completely exempt from UK income tax and CGT.

ISA typeKey features
Cash ISASavings deposits; interest tax-free; FSCS protection £85k applies

Innovative Finance ISAPeer-to-peer loans and crowdfunding; higher risk; FSCS does not cover investment losses
Lifetime ISA (LISA)Age 18–39 to open; 25% government bonus (max £1,000/year on £4,000 contribution); use for first home (property up to £450,000) or from age 60; 25% withdrawal penalty applies otherwise (loses bonus AND portion of own money effectively)
Junior ISAUnder 18; annual limit £9,000; cash or S&S; inaccessible until child reaches 18; can transfer from CTF
Annual ISA allowance 2024/25
  • Total: £20,000 per person
  • Can split across multiple ISA types
  • LISA contribution: counts toward the £20,000 (max £4,000 to LISA)
  • Junior ISA: separate £9,000 limit
  • Unused allowance cannot be carried forward
LISA Penalty Detail
  • Withdrawal penalty: 25% of withdrawal amount
  • Bonus was 25% of contribution — the 25% penalty on the grossed-up amount effectively claws back the bonus AND 6.25% of your own money
  • Penalty does NOT apply: first home purchase (up to £450k), terminal illness, death, or from age 60
Exam Trap: The LISA penalty is 25% of the withdrawal amount (not just the bonus). Because the bonus was added first (growing the pot), the 25% penalty on the larger amount exceeds the original bonus — the saver loses some of their own money if they withdraw early for an ineligible reason.

30. Endowment Assurance

A life assurance policy that also has a savings/investment element. Pays out either on death within the term OR at maturity (whichever comes first).

With-Profits Endowment
  • Invested in with-profits fund
  • Reversionary bonuses: declared annually, added to the sum assured — once added, cannot be taken away
  • Terminal bonus: discretionary lump sum added on maturity or death claim; can be substantial
  • Smoother returns than unit-linked; less volatile
Unit-Linked Endowment
  • Value linked directly to investment fund performance
  • Higher potential returns, more volatile
  • More transparent — you can see fund value daily
  • No reversionary bonuses
Low-Cost Endowment
  • Historically used to repay interest-only mortgages
  • Projected to repay the mortgage at maturity (but not guaranteed)
  • Many policies in 1990s-2000s suffered shortfalls as investment returns disappointed
  • No longer commonly sold as mortgage vehicles
Surrender & Paid-Up
  • Surrender value: cash received if policy cancelled early; likely less than premiums paid in early years
  • Paid-up policy: stop paying premiums; reduced sum assured maintained until maturity
  • Market value adjuster (MVA): with-profits fund may apply on early exit in poor market conditions

31. Qualifying Policies

Certain life assurance policies that meet HMRC's criteria are 'qualifying' — gains are exempt from income tax (and CGT) on maturity or surrender.

Requirements to qualify:

  • Regular premiums payable at least annually throughout the term
  • Minimum term: 10 years (or to age 75 if earlier)
  • Sum assured ≥ 75% of total premiums payable over the term (ensures life cover is genuine)
  • Cannot be a single-premium policy (those are investment bonds — non-qualifying)
  • Cannot be assigned for value (except on divorce settlement)
Tax treatment of qualifying policies: Any gain realised on maturity, surrender, or death claim is completely free of income tax and CGT for the policyholder. This is a significant advantage over investment bonds.
Endowment assurance policies are typically qualifying policies if they meet the above criteria. The qualifying status must be maintained — surrendering early may trigger a tax charge if HMRC's conditions weren't met throughout.

32. Investment Bonds

Single-premium, non-qualifying life assurance policies with an investment element. Commonly used for tax planning, especially by higher-rate taxpayers and those expecting to become lower-rate taxpayers in future.

FeatureDetail
5% withdrawal ruleUp to 5% of the original investment can be withdrawn each year without an immediate tax liability. Unused allowance accumulates (e.g., 3 years unused = 15% in year 4). Tax is deferred — not exempt.
Chargeable eventsDeath, full surrender, partial surrender exceeding 5% cumulative allowance, policy maturity, assignment for value. Trigger income tax assessment.
Gain calculationGain = surrender value + all withdrawals â premiums paid. Taxed as income at marginal rate.
Basic rate creditGains are treated as having had 20% basic rate tax paid. Basic rate taxpayers have no additional liability. Higher/additional rate taxpayers pay the extra.
Top-slicing reliefDivides the gain by the number of years the bond has been held to determine a 'slice'. The slice is added to income to determine the effective marginal rate. Reduces the rate applicable to the full gain.
Offshore bondsNo tax deducted within the fund (gross roll-up). On encashment: full income tax (no 20% credit). But deferral until encashment can be tax-efficient.
Critical Exam Points: (1) The 5% rule is a deferral, not an exemption — tax is eventually due. (2) Chargeable gains are taxed as income, not CGT. (3) Basic rate credit of 20% means UK bonds are grossed up at basic rate. (4) Top-slicing is a relief that may reduce effective rate — it does not exempt the gain.

33. Term Life Assurance Policies

Pure protection — pays a lump sum (or income) on death within a specified term. No investment element. No surrender value (usually).

Term vs whole-of-life: Term only pays if death occurs within the term — it is cheaper as there is no certainty of payout. Whole-of-life assurance (covered in Day 09) is certain to pay out eventually — hence higher premiums. Term is the most appropriate recommendation for mortgage protection.

ISA Key Numbers
  • Annual ISA limit: £20,000
  • LISA: max £4,000/year; 25% bonus (max £1,000)
  • LISA penalty: 25% of withdrawal; age limit to open: 18–39
  • Junior ISA: £9,000/year; accessible at 18
  • Unused ISA allowance: CANNOT carry forward
Investment Bonds (5% Rule)
  • 5% of original investment per year, deferred not exempt
  • Unused allowance accumulates
  • Chargeable events: death, surrender, excess withdrawal
  • Gains taxed as income (not CGT)
  • 20% basic rate credit; top-slicing may help HR taxpayers
Investment Trust vs OEIC/Unit Trust
  • Investment trust: closed-ended, listed, can gear, discount/premium to NAV
  • OEIC: open-ended, single price, ACD, umbrella structure
  • Unit trust: open-ended, dual price, trustee, COLL rules
Qualifying Policy vs Investment Bond
  • Qualifying: regular premiums, 10-year+ term, sum assured ≥75% premiums → gain tax-FREE
  • Investment bond: single premium, non-qualifying → 5% rule, chargeable events, income tax on gain

Practice Questions

Q1. Marcus, aged 30, contributes £4,000 to his Lifetime ISA in 2024/25. He then withdraws the entire balance to fund a car purchase. Which of the following best describes the outcome?
A. He receives the full balance back including the government bonus — no penalty for withdrawal
B. He pays a 25% penalty on the withdrawal amount, which claws back the bonus AND a portion of his own contributions
C. He pays a 25% penalty only on the government bonus received, not his own contributions
D. He loses the government bonus but receives all his own contributions back in full
Reveal answer
Answer: B. The LISA withdrawal penalty is 25% of the total withdrawal amount (including the bonus). Since the bonus was 25% of his £4,000 = £1,000, his LISA pot was £5,000. The 25% penalty on £5,000 = £1,250. He gets back £5,000 â £1,250 = £3,750 — meaning he loses his £1,000 bonus AND £250 of his own money. A car purchase does not qualify as an exempt reason (only first home up to £450k, age 60+, terminal illness, or death are exempt).
Q2. An investment trust currently trades at a 12% discount to its Net Asset Value (NAV). If the NAV per share is £2.50, at what price are the shares trading?
A. £2.80
B. £2.20
C. £2.50
D. £2.30
Reveal answer
Answer: B — £2.20. A 12% discount means the share price is 12% BELOW the NAV. £2.50 Ã (1 â 0.12) = £2.50 Ã 0.88 = £2.20. Q3. David invested £100,000 in an investment bond 5 years ago. He has taken no withdrawals. He now wishes to withdraw £30,000. What is the maximum he can withdraw without triggering an immediate chargeable event?
A. £5,000 — the annual 5% allowance for the current year only
B. £10,000 — the current year plus one year's carry-forward
C. £25,000 — five years of unused 5% allowances accumulated
D. £30,000 — any withdrawal is allowed without tax
