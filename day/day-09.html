Protection products are central to financial planning and mortgage advice. Exam tests ability to match product to client need, understand tax treatment of premiums and benefits, and identify key differences between similar products.

34. Trusts and Life Assurance 101

A trust is a legal arrangement where the settlor transfers assets to trustees to hold and manage for the benefit of beneficiaries. Writing a life assurance policy in trust is one of the most important planning tools in mortgage and protection advice.

Why write a life policy in trust?

  • Avoids probate: proceeds are paid directly to trustees/beneficiaries — no need to wait for a Grant of Probate (which can take months)
  • Outside the estate: proceeds do not form part of the deceased's estate — potentially saving 40% IHT
  • Directed to right people: trustees ensure correct beneficiaries receive the money even if the deceased left a poorly drafted will
  • Speed: bereaved family can access funds quickly to cover mortgage, funeral costs, etc.

Types of trust used with life assurance

Trust typeBeneficiariesFlexibilityUse
Absolute (Bare) trustFixed at outset — cannot changeNone — irrevocableSimple family protection where beneficiaries are certain
Discretionary trustClass of potential beneficiaries; trustees decide who, how much, whenHigh — can add or remove within classWhere family circumstances may change; IHT planning; Relevant Life policies
Split trustPolicyholder retains benefit for critical illness / terminal illness; life cover proceeds go into trustModerateCombined life + CI policies; ensures CI payout is not caught in trust
Exam Trap: If a life policy is NOT written in trust, the proceeds form part of the deceased's estate and are subject to IHT and probate delays. Writing in trust does NOT affect the tax treatment of premiums — it affects where the proceeds go on death.
Trustees' duties: Act in the best interests of beneficiaries, not the settlor. Trustee Act 2000 gives default powers of investment. Trustees must avoid conflicts of interest and keep proper accounts.

35. Relevant Life Assurance

A Relevant Life Policy (RLP) is a tax-efficient life cover arrangement where an employer takes out a life assurance policy on an employee's life, written in trust, for the benefit of the employee's dependants.

Tax advantages
  • Employer premiums are tax-deductible as a business expense (corporation tax saving)
  • Premiums are not a benefit in kind — employee pays no income tax or NI on them
  • Proceeds paid to beneficiaries are free of income tax
  • Proceeds are outside the employee's estate for IHT purposes (must be in discretionary trust)
Key features
  • Must be written under a discretionary trust
  • Cannot be assigned or surrendered by the employee
  • Does NOT count toward the Annual Allowance (unlike death-in-service via occupational pension)
  • Suitable for directors, high earners who have used pension Annual Allowance
  • Pays lump sum on death; some include terminal illness benefit
Exam Trap: Relevant Life cover does NOT count toward the pension Annual Allowance. Death-in-service written through a registered pension scheme DOES count. This distinction is frequently tested. Also: RLP must be written in trust — if it is not, the tax advantages do not apply.

36. Whole of Life Policies

Unlike term assurance, a whole of life policy guarantees a payout on death — whenever it occurs. There is no expiry date. This certainty of payout means premiums are higher than term.

Types of whole of life policy

TypeHow sum assured / premiums workKey feature
With-profitsSum assured grows as reversionary bonuses are added; terminal bonus on deathSmoothed returns; bonuses cannot be removed once added
Unit-linkedSum assured / fund value linked to investment fund performanceTransparent; more volatile; potential for higher growth
Maximum cover (non-profit)High initial sum assured; reviewed at intervals (e.g., every 10 years); premium or sum assured adjusted at reviewCheapest initially; sum assured can fall sharply at reviews if policyholder is older/unhealthier
Low-cost / flexibleMix of protection and investment elementFlexibility to adjust cover and premiums
Reviewable vs Non-reviewable
  • Non-reviewable: fixed premium throughout life; higher initially; certainty; appropriate for IHT planning where a fixed known cost is needed
  • Reviewable: lower initial premium; reviewed periodically; can increase significantly as policyholder ages — most increases happen as policyholders approach retirement when they can least afford it
Uses of whole of life
  • IHT planning: policy in trust pays IHT bill on death; sum assured = anticipated IHT liability
  • Funeral costs
  • Leaving a lump sum legacy to family
  • Shareholder/partnership protection (combined with legal agreements)
Exam Trap: Reviewable whole of life policies can result in significantly increased premiums at review, or a reduced sum assured — the client must understand this risk. Non-reviewable (guaranteed) policies cost more upfront but provide certainty — critical for IHT planning where the liability is predictable.

37. Income Protection Insurance (IPI)

IPI (also called Permanent Health Insurance / PHI) pays a regular tax-free income if the policyholder is unable to work due to illness or injury. It is designed to replace earned income until recovery, retirement, or death.

Key features
  • Benefit amount: typically 50–65% of pre-disability earnings (to retain work incentive)
  • Deferred period: waiting period before benefit starts — 4, 13, 26, or 52 weeks; longer deferred = lower premium
  • Benefit payment period: continues until recovery, retirement date, or death
  • Long-term: unlike ASU, IPI can pay for decades
  • Own premiums paid by individual = benefit tax-free; if employer pays = benefit is taxable
Definition of disability — 3 levels
  • Own occupation: unable to do YOUR specific job — most generous, most expensive; ideal for specialist professionals (e.g., surgeon, pilot)
  • Suited occupation: unable to do a job suited to your skills, education, and experience — mid-level
  • Any occupation: unable to do ANY job at all — most restrictive, cheapest; rarely appropriate for professional clients
Waiver of premium: Many IPI policies include a waiver of premium benefit — if the policyholder is on a claim, premiums are waived. This is critical because if they couldn't pay premiums during disability, the policy would lapse when they need it most.
Exam Trap: IPI does NOT cover unemployment — only illness and injury. If a client needs redundancy cover, they need ASU. The deferred period should be aligned with any employer sick pay period (e.g., if employer pays 26 weeks sick pay, choose a 26-week deferred period).

38. Critical Illness Cover (CIC)

CIC pays a tax-free lump sum on diagnosis of a specified serious illness. It pays while the policyholder is still alive — on diagnosis, not on death.

Commonly covered conditions

  • Cancer (of specified severity)
  • Heart attack (of specified severity)
  • Stroke (with permanent symptoms)
  • Coronary artery bypass surgery
  • Multiple sclerosis
  • Kidney failure requiring dialysis
  • Organ transplant (as recipient)
  • Total permanent disability
  • Terminal illness (where life expectancy < 12 months)
Policy types
  • Standalone CIC: only pays on CI — no death benefit
  • Accelerated CIC: combined with life cover; CI claim reduces (or eliminates) life sum assured — cheaper than having both separately
  • Rider benefit: CIC added to a life policy as an add-on
  • Level, decreasing, or increasing sum assured options
Key conditions
  • Survival period: must survive a specified period (typically 14–28 days) after diagnosis before benefit is paid
  • Pre-existing conditions: typically excluded
  • Partial payments: some policies pay reduced amounts for early-stage cancers or less severe heart attacks
  • Self-inflicted injury excluded
Exam Trap: CIC pays on DIAGNOSIS — not on death. A client might receive a CIC payout and still recover fully. If the policy is accelerated and linked to life cover, the life sum assured is reduced or eliminated when a CI claim is paid. Distinguish from IPI (income) vs CIC (lump sum).

39. Private Medical Insurance (PMI)

PMI covers the cost of private medical treatment. It does not replace income — it covers hospital fees, consultant fees, and treatment costs outside the NHS.

Cover levelWhat's included
ComprehensiveInpatient, day-patient, and outpatient treatment; specialist consultations; diagnostic tests; private hospital room
Core / EssentialInpatient and day-patient only; no routine outpatient
BudgetLimited list of conditions/hospitals; lowest premiums
Underwriting methods
  • Full Medical Underwriting (FMU): detailed health declaration at outset; pre-existing conditions excluded permanently (or for stated period)
  • Moratorium: no health questions at outset; pre-existing conditions excluded for first 2 years; covered after 2 years if not suffered from in that period — simpler application process
  • Continued Personal Medical Exclusions (CPME): used when switching insurer; mirrors previous policy exclusions
Key points
  • Insurance Premium Tax (IPT): PMI premiums attract IPT at 12% (standard rate applies to health insurance)
  • Employer-provided PMI is a taxable benefit in kind (P11D benefit)
  • PMI does NOT replace income — combine with IPI for comprehensive protection
  • Group schemes (employer): usually cheaper than individual policy; underwriting may be relaxed for large groups
Exam Trap: PMI only covers treatment costs — not lost earnings. A client off work for 6 months following surgery still needs IPI for income. Also: employer-provided PMI is a P11D benefit-in-kind — the employee pays income tax on the premium value.

40. General Insurance Protection

General insurance provides protection against financial loss from unexpected events — accidents, damage, liability. Unlike life assurance, general insurance policies are typically annual contracts renewed each year.

  • Buildings insurance: covers the structure of the property (walls, roof, floors, permanent fixtures); usually required by mortgage lenders; sum insured should equal the rebuild cost, not market value
  • Contents insurance: covers moveable possessions within the home; often separately insured to buildings
  • Accidental damage: optional add-on; covers accidental damage to buildings or contents not caused by a defined peril
  • Legal expenses insurance: covers cost of pursuing or defending legal claims
  • Personal accident insurance: pays lump sum for accidental death, permanent disablement, or loss of limb/sight
Indemnity principle: General insurance operates on indemnity — the insurer pays only to restore the insured to the same financial position as before the loss. You cannot profit from a general insurance claim.

41. ASU vs IPI — Key Distinctions

ASU (Accident, Sickness and Unemployment) is short-term income protection covering three risks: accident, sickness, and unemployment/redundancy. It is quite different from IPI despite both protecting income.

FeatureASUIPI (Income Protection Insurance)
Maximum benefit period12–24 months per claimUntil retirement, recovery, or death
Unemployment coverYES — includes redundancyNO — illness/injury only
Deferred periodUsually 30–90 days4, 13, 26, or 52 weeks
Definition of incapacityUsually own occupation (short-term)Own / suited / any occupation (key choice)
Typical benefitUp to 65% of income; max often £1,500–£2,000/monthUp to 65% of pre-disability income
RegulationFCA regulated; historically linked to PPIFCA regulated
PPI linkPayment Protection Insurance (PPI) is a form of ASU linked to a specific credit product — widely mis-sold historicallyNo PPI equivalent
Exam Trap: ASU is the only product that covers unemployment/redundancy. IPI does NOT cover unemployment. For a client who is an employee with a mortgage and concerned about redundancy, only ASU provides that protection. However, for long-term illness risk (disability lasting years), IPI is far superior as ASU stops paying after 12–24 months.

Exam Traps — Day 09

  • Trust + IHT: Writing a life policy in trust takes proceeds outside the estate — saving 40% IHT — AND avoids probate delays. Both benefits, not just one.
  • Relevant Life: NOT a benefit in kind; premiums are corporation tax deductible; proceeds outside estate; does NOT count toward pension Annual Allowance.
  • Whole of life reviewable: Reviewable policies can lead to premium increases or sum assured reductions at review — not suitable where a fixed known cost is essential (IHT planning). Non-reviewable = certainty.
  • IPI deferred period: Match to employer sick pay period — no point starting benefit payment before employer sick pay ends.
  • CIC survival period: Must survive typically 14–28 days post-diagnosis. Terminal illness benefit may override this.
  • PMI = taxable BIK: Employer-provided PMI is a P11D benefit; employee pays income tax on the premium value.
  • ASU vs IPI: Only ASU covers redundancy. Only IPI is genuinely long-term (to retirement).

Memorise

Trust benefits
  • Outside estate → no IHT (40% saving)
  • Avoids probate → speed
  • Directed to correct beneficiaries
  • Absolute = fixed beneficiaries; Discretionary = flexible
  • Split trust: policyholder keeps CI; life goes into trust
Relevant Life
  • Employer pays, employee gets cover
  • Not a P11D benefit
  • Premiums: corporation tax deductible
  • Proceeds: IHT exempt (in discretionary trust)
  • Does NOT count toward pension Annual Allowance
IPI vs ASU
  • IPI: long-term, no unemployment, own/suited/any occ
  • ASU: short-term (12–24 months), includes unemployment
  • IPI benefit = tax-free (if individual pays premiums)
  • IPI deferred = match employer sick pay
CIC key facts
  • Pays on DIAGNOSIS not death
  • Survival period: 14–28 days
  • Accelerated = reduces life cover on claim
  • Standalone = no life cover element
  • Pre-existing conditions excluded

Practice Questions

Q1. Sarah has a whole of life policy with reviewable premiums taken out 15 years ago to cover an anticipated inheritance tax liability. At the last review, her insurer significantly increased her premium. Which alternative product would have better suited her need from the outset?
A. An ASU policy with critical illness rider
B. A non-reviewable (guaranteed) whole of life policy written in discretionary trust
C. A decreasing term assurance policy
D. An Income Protection Insurance policy
Reveal answer
Answer: B. For IHT planning, the key requirement is a guaranteed payout of a known sum at a predictable fixed premium throughout life. A non-reviewable whole of life policy in discretionary trust meets all these criteria — the premiums never increase and the sum assured is guaranteed to pay the IHT bill on death. Reviewable policies are unsuitable for IHT planning precisely because the premium uncertainty undermines the financial planning. Term assurance (C) is wrong because IHT is due on death regardless of when it occurs — term cover could expire before death.
Q2. James is a self-employed architect. He is considering income protection and wants to ensure that if he becomes unable to perform his specific professional duties, he can claim — even if he could theoretically do other work. Which definition of disability should his policy contain?
A. Any occupation
B. Suited occupation
C. Own occupation
D. Total permanent disability
Reveal answer
Answer: C — Own occupation. The own occupation definition pays if the policyholder cannot perform the duties of THEIR specific job — regardless of whether they could do other work. For a professional like an architect, this is the most appropriate and most generous definition. "Suited occupation" would allow the insurer to refuse a claim if James could theoretically work as an architectural consultant or draughtsman. "Any occupation" is the most restrictive and would only pay if James could not do any job at all — highly inappropriate for a professional.
Q3. Which of the following best explains why a Relevant Life Policy is advantageous compared to an employer providing life cover through a registered group pension scheme?
A. Relevant Life premiums attract tax relief at the employee's marginal rate
B. Relevant Life cover counts toward the employee's pension Annual Allowance, reducing taxable contributions
C. Relevant Life cover does not count toward the employee's pension Annual Allowance, making it useful for employees who have used their full allowance
D. Relevant Life policies pay on diagnosis of critical illness as well as death
Reveal answer
Answer: C. Death-in-service benefits provided through a registered pension scheme count toward the Annual Allowance (currently £60,000 for 2024/25). For high-earning employees who have used their full Annual Allowance, any death-in-service via the pension scheme would cause an Annual Allowance breach and a tax charge. Relevant Life cover sits outside the pension regime — the premiums are paid by the employer as a tax-deductible business expense, not as a pension contribution — so there is no impact on the Annual Allowance. Answer A is wrong — it is the employer (company) that gets the deduction, not the employee personally.
