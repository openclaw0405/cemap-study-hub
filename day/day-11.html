Day 11 — The Financial Planning Process

Ch3 Topics 1–3: Financial Life Cycle · Gathering Information · Contents of a Factfind
nning: understanding where a client is in their life, gathering the right information, and completing a factfind. Small chapter — but factfind content and the distinction between hard/soft facts appear repeatedly in exams.

CeMAP 1Ch3 scope

Only 3 topics but examiners test the categories of factfind information heavily. Know the life cycle stages and which products are appropriate at each stage. Know the difference between quantitative and qualitative information.

1. Financial Life Cycle

Clients' financial needs change at different life stages. Advisers must map product recommendations to where the client sits in their life cycle.

Life StageTypical CharacteristicsCommon Financial Needs
Young single (18–25)Low income, few commitments, possible student debtBudgeting, emergency fund, start pension contributions, protection basics
Young couple / pre-childrenDual income, saving for deposit, increasing lifestyleMortgage planning, life assurance, savings, begin ISA contributions
Family with childrenMortgage, childcare costs, reduced disposable incomeAdequate life cover, income protection, critical illness, children's savings
Middle age / children leavingHigher earnings, mortgage reducing, children grownAccelerate pension contributions, review protection needs (may reduce), IHT planning
Pre-retirement (55–65)Peak earnings, pension pots accumulating, approaching access agePension consolidation, drawdown planning, IHT, equity release consideration
RetirementNo employment income, relying on pension/savings/state pensionIncome sustainability, annuity vs drawdown, LPA, estate planning, care costs
Key principle: The need for life cover typically reduces as mortgage is repaid and children leave home. The need for long-term care planning and IHT planning typically increases in later stages. Advisers should always align recommendations to the client's current life stage — not assume a one-size-fits-all approach.

2. Gathering Information

Before giving advice, an adviser must gather comprehensive information about the client. The FCA requires that advice is suitable — and suitability depends on knowing the client thoroughly.

Quantitative (Hard) Information
  • Objective, factual, numerical data
  • Income (salary, rental, benefits, pension)
  • Expenditure (mortgage payment, bills, regular outgoings)
  • Assets (savings, property, pensions, investments)
  • Liabilities (mortgage balance, loans, credit cards, overdrafts)
  • Existing policies (life cover sums assured, premiums, surrender values)
  • Tax position (income tax band, CGT position)
Qualitative (Soft) Information
  • Subjective, attitudinal, personal data
  • Attitude to risk (risk appetite and capacity for loss)
  • Goals and aspirations (what does the client want to achieve?)
  • Health status (impacts life cover, income protection, annuity rates)
  • Family circumstances (dependants, caring responsibilities)
  • Employment status and job security
  • Values and preferences (ethical investment, ESG considerations)
Exam Trap: Both types of information are required for a suitability assessment. The FCA's suitability rules (under COBS and MCOB) require advisers to obtain sufficient information on the client's financial situation, investment objectives AND knowledge and experience. An adviser cannot give advice on partial information — this could constitute an unsuitability breach. Attitude to risk is a qualitative (soft) fact, NOT a quantitative one.
KYC — Know Your Client: The process of gathering and verifying client information. Also feeds into Anti-Money Laundering (AML) obligations — advisers must verify identity (passport, driving licence) and address (utility bill, bank statement) under the Money Laundering Regulations. Advisers must apply Customer Due Diligence (CDD) to all new clients.

3. Contents of a Factfind

A factfind is the structured document (paper or electronic) used to record all client information gathered during the initial meeting. It forms the foundation for the suitability report and must be kept on file.

SectionInformation Collected
Personal detailsFull name, date of birth, address, contact details, NI number, marital status, dependants
Employment and incomeEmployer, occupation, salary, bonus, benefits in kind, self-employment income, rental income, state benefits received
ExpenditureMortgage/rent, household bills, food, transport, insurances, loan repayments, regular commitments
AssetsProperty values, savings accounts, investments (ISAs, bonds, stocks), pension values, business interests
LiabilitiesMortgage balance and type, personal loans, credit cards, hire purchase, guarantees given
Protection provisionsExisting life cover, critical illness, income protection (sum assured, premiums, terms, in-trust?)
Pension provisionsCurrent and deferred pensions, employer schemes, state pension forecast, planned retirement age
Tax positionIncome tax band, whether self-assessed, CGT position, IHT exposure, trusts
Attitude to riskRisk profile (cautious / balanced / adventurous), capacity for loss, investment time horizon
Health and lifestyleSmoker/non-smoker, known medical conditions (relevant for protection and annuity), disabilities
Objectives and prioritiesImmediate objectives (e.g., buy a house, clear debt), medium-term (children's education, protection review), long-term (retirement income, estate planning)
Existing arrangements reviewSummary of all existing financial products and whether they remain suitable
Exam Trap: The factfind is NOT simply a form-filling exercise — it is a legally significant document. If an adviser recommends a product that is unsuitable and the factfind shows the adviser had (or should have had) information pointing to unsuitability, the adviser faces regulatory action and potential compensation liability. Factfinds must be kept for at least 5 years (mortgage advice: 3 years minimum; investments: 5 years minimum under FCA retention rules).
Capacity for Loss vs Attitude to Risk: These are different. Attitude to risk = how the client feels about risk (psychological tolerance). Capacity for loss = how much loss the client can financially afford to sustain (objective financial resilience). A client may have a high attitude to risk but low capacity for loss (e.g., all savings needed within 2 years for a house purchase). The adviser must use the lower of the two as the binding constraint.

Practice Questions

Q1. An adviser is completing a factfind with a new client, Priya. Priya says she loves taking investment risk and considers herself adventurous. However, on reviewing her finances, the adviser notes that all of Priya's savings (£25,000) will be needed within 18 months to fund a house purchase deposit. Which of the following best describes the correct approach?
A. Recommend adventurous investments, as Priya's stated attitude to risk must be respected
B. Recommend cautious / capital-protected investments, as Priya's capacity for loss is low regardless of her attitude to risk
C. Split the portfolio 50/50 between adventurous and cautious funds as a compromise
D. Refer Priya to a specialist risk adviser before proceeding
Reveal answer
Answer: B. Attitude to risk (ATR) and capacity for loss are two distinct concepts. ATR is Priya's psychological/emotional tolerance for risk. Capacity for loss is her objective financial ability to sustain losses. Because Priya needs the full £25,000 within 18 months for a house purchase, she has very low capacity for loss — she cannot afford to lose any material amount. The adviser must use the more conservative constraint. Even though Priya wants to take risk, recommending adventurous investments would be unsuitable given her financial circumstances. The FCA suitability rules require advisers to account for both dimensions.
Q2. Which of the following items would be classified as QUALITATIVE (soft) information on a client factfind?
A. The outstanding balance on the client's mortgage
B. The client's annual salary and bonus
C. The client's attitude to investment risk and long-term goals
D. The surrender value of an existing endowment policy
Reveal answer
Answer: C. Qualitative (soft) information is subjective and attitudinal — it captures how a client thinks, feels, and prioritises. Attitude to risk and long-term goals are classic examples of qualitative data. Options A, B, and D are all quantitative (hard) facts — they are objective, numerical, and measurable. The distinction matters because both types are required for a suitability assessment; an adviser who only collects hard facts but ignores soft facts cannot properly assess whether a recommendation is suitable for the client's goals and risk profile.
Q3. Under FCA rules, what is the minimum period for which an adviser must retain records relating to investment advice given to a retail client?
A. 1 year from the date of the advice
B. 3 years from the date of the advice
C. 5 years from the date of the advice
D. 10 years from the date of the advice
Reveal answer
Answer: C. FCA rules (COBS 9.5) require firms to retain records of investment advice — including the suitability report and factfind — for a minimum of 5 years from the date the advice was given. For mortgage advice, the minimum is 3 years (MCOB). However, for pension transfer advice, regulators expect records to be retained for much longer given the potential for deferred complaints. In the exam context, the standard answer for investment advice is 5 years. The factfind is a key part of this retention requirement — it evidences the basis on which suitability was assessed.
