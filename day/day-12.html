CeMAP 1Ch4 scope

11 legal concepts covering the full lifecycle of client affairs: death and succession, business structures, contract formation, insurance disclosure duties, financial crime, property ownership, incapacity planning, and insolvency. Many topics link directly to mortgage and protection advice practice.

1. Personal Representatives

When a person dies, someone must administer their estate — collect assets, pay debts, and distribute to beneficiaries. The generic term is Personal Representative (PR).

Executor
  • Named in a valid Will to administer the estate
  • Appointed by the deceased (the testator)
  • Applies for a Grant of Probate from the Probate Registry to obtain legal authority to act
  • Can act immediately on death but Grant of Probate needed to deal with most assets (banks, Land Registry)
Administrator
  • Appointed when there is NO valid Will (intestacy), or no executor named/willing to act
  • Appointed by the court under the Administration of Estates Act 1925
  • Applies for Letters of Administration (not Grant of Probate)
  • Usually the closest next of kin (spouse, then children, then parents...)
Probate threshold: Most financial institutions require a Grant of Probate before releasing funds. For small estates (typically under £5,000–£10,000 — threshold varies by institution), some banks release funds without Probate. Property held as joint tenants passes by survivorship automatically — no Probate needed for that asset alone.
Exam Trap: Executor = named in a Will → Grant of Probate. Administrator = no Will (or no executor available) → Letters of Administration. These are different legal documents. An executor can be an individual or a professional (solicitor, bank trust company). Personal representatives have both powers AND personal liability — they can be held personally liable for distributing assets incorrectly before paying debts.

2. Wills

A Will is a legal document expressing the testator's wishes regarding distribution of their estate on death. Making a Will is the only way to control who inherits.

Validity requirements (Wills Act 1837)

  • Must be in writing
  • Must be signed by the testator (or someone at their direction and in their presence)
  • Signature must be made or acknowledged in the presence of two independent witnesses present at the same time
  • Both witnesses must sign in the testator's presence
  • Testator must have testamentary capacity (be of sound mind) and be 18 or over (or a married minor)
Key Will terms
  • Testator: The person making the Will
  • Beneficiary: A person who receives a gift under a Will
  • Legatee: Recipient of personal property (legacy)
  • Devisee: Recipient of real property (land)
  • Residuary estate: Everything left after specific gifts and debts are settled
  • Codicil: A written amendment to a Will — must also be witnessed
When a Will becomes invalid / revoked
  • Marriage or civil partnership (automatically revokes an existing Will)
  • Divorce does NOT revoke the Will — but former spouse is treated as having predeceased for inheritance purposes
  • Physical destruction by the testator with intent to revoke
  • A subsequent valid Will (revokes earlier Wills in full or part)
  • A beneficiary or their spouse witnesses the Will → that beneficiary's gift fails (but Will remains valid)
Exam Trap: Marriage automatically revokes an existing Will. Divorce does NOT — but the ex-spouse is treated as having predeceased (their gifts and executor appointment lapse). A witness to a Will cannot benefit from it — if a beneficiary witnesses, their gift fails but the Will itself remains valid. Two witnesses are required — both present simultaneously, both sign in the testator's presence.

3. Intestacy

If a person dies without a valid Will (or their Will does not cover all their estate), the intestacy rules under the Administration of Estates Act 1925 (as amended by the Inheritance and Trustees' Powers Act 2014) determine who inherits.

Surviving family situationWho inherits under intestacy (England & Wales)
Spouse/civil partner only (no children)Spouse/CP inherits entire estate
Spouse/CP + childrenSpouse/CP: first £322,000 (statutory legacy 2023+) + personal chattels + half of remainder. Children share the other half equally.
Children only (no spouse/CP)Children share estate equally; if a child predeceases but has children, those grandchildren take that child's share
No spouse/CP, no childrenParents → siblings → half-siblings → grandparents → aunts/uncles → half-aunts/uncles → Crown (bona vacantia)
No surviving relatives at allEstate passes to the Crown (bona vacantia)
Exam Trap: Unmarried partners (cohabitees), however long together, receive NOTHING under intestacy rules. Only a legally married spouse or civil partner benefits. This is one of the strongest arguments for making a Will. Also: the statutory legacy (£322,000 in 2023) and the half-share apply — children do NOT inherit everything just because the spouse is alive. Adopted children have the same rights as biological children; stepchildren with no legal adoption have no automatic intestacy rights.
Scotland has different rules — Prior Rights and Legal Rights operate under Scots law. CeMAP primarily tests England & Wales rules but be aware the exam may flag Scottish differences.

4. Sole Traders, Partnerships and Charities

Sole Trader
  • Single person running a business
  • No legal separation between individual and business
  • Unlimited personal liability — personal assets at risk for business debts
  • Simple setup; no formal registration required (just HMRC notification)
  • All profits taxed as personal income (income tax + NI)
Partnership
  • Two or more partners running a business together
  • Governed by Partnership Act 1890 (default) or written partnership agreement
  • Unlimited joint and several liability — each partner personally liable for ALL partnership debts (including those created by other partners)
  • Limited Partnership: at least one general partner (unlimited liability) + at least one limited partner (liability limited to capital invested; cannot manage business)
  • LLP (Limited Liability Partnership): separate legal entity; partners' liability limited to agreed contribution; must register at Companies House; required to file accounts
Charity
  • Must be registered with the Charity Commission (if income >£5,000)
  • Operates for public benefit — cannot distribute profits to members
  • Regulated by the Charities Act 2011
  • Tax advantages: Gift Aid on donations (basic rate tax reclaimed); 80% mandatory business rates relief; exemption from most taxes on charitable income
  • Trustees have legal responsibility for governance — volunteer responsibility
Exam Trap: In an ordinary partnership, joint and several liability means a creditor can pursue ANY one partner for the full debt — not just their share. LLP provides limited liability AND is a separate legal entity (unlike a standard limited partnership). Limited partners in a limited partnership cannot participate in management — if they do, they lose their limited liability status.

5. Contract Law

A legally binding contract requires six elements:

Essential elements of a valid contract
  1. Offer: A clear, definite proposal to be bound on specific terms
  2. Acceptance: Unconditional agreement to all terms of the offer (a counter-offer kills the original offer)
  3. Consideration: Something of value given by each party (e.g., premium paid; insurer accepts risk)
  4. Intention to create legal relations: Presumed in commercial/business contexts; rebutted in domestic/social contexts
  5. Capacity: Both parties must have legal capacity (18+; of sound mind; not under duress)
  6. Legality: Contract must not be for an illegal purpose
Vitiating factors (make contract voidable or void)
  • Misrepresentation: False statement of fact inducing contract (innocent, negligent, or fraudulent)
  • Mistake: Common mistake (both parties wrong about fundamental fact)
  • Duress / undue influence: Pressure or improper influence removing free will
  • Illegality: Contract for illegal purposes — void ab initio
  • Lack of capacity: Minor (under 18) — contract voidable; minors can enforce against adult but not vice versa
In insurance contracts: The contract is formed when the insurer accepts the risk (not when the proposal form is submitted). Offer = proposal form submitted by the applicant. Acceptance = insurer accepts the risk (may be conditional on survey/medical). Cover note provides immediate temporary cover pending formal policy issue.

6. Consumer Insurance Act 2012

Replaced the duty of utmost good faith for consumer insurance with a more proportionate standard. Applies to insurance contracts entered into by individuals for non-business purposes.

Old law (pre-2012)
  • Consumer had a duty of utmost good faith — must volunteer ALL material information
  • Any non-disclosure (even innocent) could allow insurer to avoid the contract entirely — refuse all claims and return premiums
  • Very harsh on consumers who forgot to mention facts
Consumer Insurance Act 2012
  • Consumer must take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation
  • No duty to volunteer information — must answer questions honestly and accurately
  • Misrepresentation classified by degree of fault:
  • Innocent misrepresentation: Insurer can only avoid if it would NOT have offered cover at all; otherwise must pay claim (possibly with reduced settlement)
  • Careless misrepresentation: Insurer can avoid and refuse claim, but must return premiums
  • Deliberate or reckless misrepresentation: Insurer can avoid, keep premiums, and report to fraud databases
Exam Trap: The CIA 2012 only applies to CONSUMER insurance (individuals buying for personal use). Business insurance is still governed by the Insurance Act 2015 (which also reformed commercial insurance). The consumer's duty is now reactive — answer questions honestly — NOT proactive volunteering of all material facts. However, answering questions dishonestly or recklessly remains a serious breach with severe consequences (policy avoided, premiums kept).

7. Agency Law

An agent acts on behalf of a principal to create legal relations with third parties. In financial services, agency relationships are fundamental — advisers, brokers, and intermediaries are agents.

Key Agency concepts
  • Principal: The party on whose behalf the agent acts
  • Agent: Acts on behalf of the principal; their acts bind the principal
  • Third party: The other party who deals with the agent
  • Actual authority: Express (given explicitly) or implied (reasonably flows from role)
  • Apparent (ostensible) authority: Principal represents to third party that agent has authority — even if agent exceeds actual authority, principal may be bound
  • Ratification: Principal retrospectively approves an act done without authority
In financial services
  • Tied agent: Acts for ONE product provider only; provider is responsible for the agent's conduct
  • Multi-tied agent: Can recommend products from a panel of specified providers
  • Independent adviser (IFA): Acts in the client's best interest; no restriction on providers; considers whole of market
  • Mortgage intermediary: Owes duty of care to the client (borrower); lender is the principal for some purposes
  • Appointed Representative (AR): Carries out regulated activities under the FCA authorisation of a principal firm (the network)
Exam Trap: When an agent has apparent authority (even if not actual authority), the principal can be bound to the contract with the third party — if the principal created the impression of authority. An AR acts under the regulatory authorisation of the principal firm — the principal firm is responsible for the AR's compliance. An IFA owes their duty of care to the client, not the product provider.

8. Financial Scams

The FCA and Government regulate and educate consumers to combat financial fraud. Common types tested in CeMAP:

Exam Trap: Pension liberation scams specifically target people under 55 with promises of 'unlocking' pension funds early — this is not legally possible (unless the member is in serious ill health), and the funds received are subject to 55% unauthorised payment tax charge PLUS the fraudsters take a significant 'fee'. HMRC pursues the individual, not the fraudster.

9. Ownership of Property

Property (real estate) in England & Wales can be owned jointly in two distinct legal ways. The distinction has critical implications for inheritance and mortgage planning.

Joint Tenancy
  • Both/all co-owners own the whole property together — no distinct shares
  • Right of survivorship (jus accrescendi): On death of one joint tenant, their interest passes automatically to the surviving joint tenant(s) — regardless of Will or intestacy rules
  • Cannot leave your share under a Will — survivorship overrides
  • Common for married couples / civil partners
  • One party can sever the joint tenancy unilaterally by serving written notice — converting to tenancy in common
Tenancy in Common
  • Each co-owner holds a distinct, separate share (e.g., 50/50 or unequal shares)
  • No right of survivorship — on death, the deceased's share passes according to their Will or intestacy rules
  • Owners can leave their share to whoever they choose in their Will
  • Common where parties want to protect their individual contributions (e.g., unmarried couples, business partners, second marriages)
  • Used in IHT planning: each spouse leaves their share to a trust / children to use the Nil Rate Band
Exam Trap: Joint tenancy means survivorship ALWAYS overrides the Will. If a couple own as joint tenants, one cannot leave their half to their children — it automatically passes to the survivor. For IHT planning purposes, converting to tenancy in common allows each spouse to use their Nil Rate Band (£325,000) on first death. This is a classic exam scenario: why recommend changing from joint tenancy to tenancy in common.

10. Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA)

A Lasting Power of Attorney allows an individual (the donor) to appoint one or more attorneys to make decisions on their behalf if they lose mental capacity. Governed by the Mental Capacity Act 2005; registered with the Office of the Public Guardian (OPG).

Property and Financial Affairs LPA
  • Covers: bank accounts, investments, paying bills, selling property, managing pension income
  • Can be used WHILE the donor still has capacity (if they choose) — with donor's permission
  • Or restricted to use only when capacity is lost
  • Critical for mortgage advisers — an attorney can manage mortgage payments and property transactions
Health and Welfare LPA
  • Covers: medical treatment decisions, care home placement, daily routine, end-of-life decisions
  • Can ONLY be used when the donor has lost mental capacity — NEVER while donor still has capacity
  • The attorney can consent to or refuse medical treatment on the donor's behalf
  • Can include a specific authority to refuse life-sustaining treatment
Registration requirement: An LPA must be registered with the OPG BEFORE it can be used. Registration currently takes several weeks and costs £82 per LPA (2024 fee). Without registration, the document is legally ineffective — even if signed. Both types must be registered separately.
Exam Trap: The Health and Welfare LPA can ONLY be used when the donor lacks capacity — it cannot be used for convenience while the donor is well. The Property and Financial Affairs LPA CAN be used while the donor still has capacity (if the donor permits). There are TWO separate LPAs — many clients only register one type, leaving a gap. An Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA — old form, pre-2007) only covers property and financial affairs and could only be used if the donor lost capacity. EPAs made before 2007 remain valid.

11. Insolvency and Bankruptcy

Bankruptcy (individuals)
  • A legal status for individuals who cannot pay their debts
  • Petition can be made by the debtor OR creditors (owed £5,000+)
  • Court appoints a Trustee in Bankruptcy to realise assets and distribute to creditors
  • Duration: Usually discharged automatically after 12 months
  • Effects: credit rating severely damaged; cannot be company director; cannot obtain credit >£500 without disclosing status; may lose home if equity exceeds a threshold
  • Exempt assets: tools of trade (essential for earning income), basic household goods
  • Debt Relief Order (DRO): Cheaper alternative for those with debts under £30,000 and minimal assets (assets <£2,000; income surplus <£75/month)
Corporate Insolvency
  • Liquidation (winding up): Company assets realised, debts paid, company dissolved. Voluntary (members'/creditors') or compulsory (court order)
  • Administration: Administrator appointed to rescue company as going concern or achieve better outcome than immediate liquidation. Moratorium on creditor action.
  • Receivership: Appointed by secured creditor (debenture holder) to recover secured debt
  • Company Voluntary Arrangement (CVA): Agreement between company and unsecured creditors to restructure/repay debts — company continues trading
Order of creditor priority in insolvency:
  1. Fixed charge creditors (e.g., mortgage lender with charge over property)
  2. Insolvency practitioner costs
  3. Preferential creditors (employees' arrears of wages up to £800; holiday pay)
  4. Floating charge holders (e.g., bank with a floating charge over stock)
  5. Unsecured creditors (trade creditors, HMRC for most taxes)
  6. Shareholders / members (last — usually receive nothing)
Exam Trap: In personal bankruptcy, the bankrupt is automatically discharged after 12 months (unless misconduct). Secured creditors (e.g., mortgage lenders) are largely unaffected by bankruptcy — they retain their security over the property. Unsecured creditors may receive very little. A DRO is for low-income, low-asset individuals with debts under £30,000 — it is NOT a bankruptcy order but has similar effects (12-month moratorium; debts written off).

Exam Traps — Day 12

  • Executor vs Administrator: Executor (Will exists) → Grant of Probate. Administrator (no Will) → Letters of Administration.
  • Marriage revokes a Will; divorce does NOT — but ex-spouse loses their gifts/appointment.
  • Intestacy — cohabitees get nothing: Unmarried partners have no automatic intestacy rights regardless of length of relationship.
  • Intestacy statutory legacy: Spouse/CP gets first £322,000 + personal chattels + half of remainder; children take the other half.
  • Consumer Insurance Act 2012: Consumer must answer questions honestly (reactive duty) — no duty to volunteer. Deliberate misrepresentation = insurer keeps premiums; innocent = must still pay but may reduce settlement.
  • Joint tenancy survivorship overrides the Will — cannot leave joint tenancy share by Will. Tenancy in common: can leave share by Will (used in IHT planning).
  • Health & Welfare LPA: only usable when capacity lost. Property & Financial Affairs LPA: can be used while capacity retained (if donor permits).
  • LPA must be registered with OPG before use — unregistered LPA is legally ineffective.
  • Bankruptcy discharge: 12 months. DRO threshold: debts <£30,000; assets <£2,000; surplus income <£75/month.
  • Pension liberation scams: HMRC pursues the victim (not just the fraudster) for the 55% unauthorised payment charge.

Practice Questions

Q1. Ahmed and his long-term girlfriend Chloe have lived together for 15 years. Ahmed dies without making a Will. He has an estate worth £250,000 (savings and investments — no jointly held property). Under the intestacy rules, who inherits?
A. Chloe inherits the entire estate, as they have lived together for more than 2 years
B. Chloe inherits half the estate; Ahmed's parents inherit the other half
C. Ahmed's closest blood relatives inherit under the intestacy rules; Chloe receives nothing
D. The estate passes to the Crown as Ahmed had no Will
Reveal answer
Answer: C. Under the intestacy rules (Administration of Estates Act 1925, as amended), an unmarried cohabiting partner has NO automatic right to inherit, regardless of the length of the relationship. Only a legally married spouse or civil partner has protected inheritance rights under intestacy. Ahmed's estate would pass to his closest blood relatives — if his parents are alive, they would inherit; if not, it would pass to siblings, and so on down the statutory order. Chloe would receive nothing. This is one of the most compelling reasons for unmarried couples to make Wills. The 2-year cohabitation figure applies to potential claims under the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975, which allows cohabitees to apply to court for reasonable financial provision — but this is not an automatic right.
Q2. Maria and her sister own a house as tenants in common (50/50 shares). Maria dies and her Will leaves all her assets to her husband, Paulo. What happens to Maria's share of the house?
A. Maria's share automatically passes to her sister under the right of survivorship
B. Maria's share passes to Paulo under her Will
C. The house must be sold and the proceeds split equally between Paulo and Maria's sister
D. The house passes entirely to Maria's sister as the surviving co-owner
Reveal answer
Answer: B. Tenancy in common means each co-owner holds a distinct, separate share which can be left by Will or passed under intestacy rules. There is no right of survivorship in a tenancy in common — this is what distinguishes it from joint tenancy. Maria's 50% share passes under her Will to Paulo. Paulo now owns 50% of the house with Maria's sister (who retains her 50%). They may agree to sell or one may buy out the other, but there is no automatic sale. If the property had been held as joint tenants, Answer A would be correct — Maria's share would pass automatically to her sister by survivorship, overriding the Will entirely.
Q3. James has a Property and Financial Affairs LPA registered with the Office of the Public Guardian, naming his daughter Sophie as attorney. James is still mentally capable. Sophie wishes to begin managing James's bank account and paying bills on his behalf, as James finds it increasingly inconvenient. Is Sophie legally able to act?
A. No — an LPA can only be used after the donor loses mental capacity
B. Yes — a Property and Financial Affairs LPA can be used while the donor still has capacity, provided the donor has given permission
C. No — Sophie would need to apply to the Court of Protection for authorisation first
D. Yes — once an LPA is registered, the attorney can act unconditionally at any time
Reveal answer
Answer: B. A Property and Financial Affairs LPA can be used while the donor (James) still has mental capacity, provided the donor has consented. This flexibility makes it useful for convenience as well as incapacity planning. However, the Health and Welfare LPA is strictly the opposite — it can only be used when the donor has lost mental capacity; it cannot be used while the donor is capable, even with their permission. Answer D is incorrect — acting without the donor's permission (or outside the LPA terms) while the donor has capacity could amount to misuse of the LPA. The LPA document itself and any conditions attached must be checked. Sophie needs James's current permission to act while he still has capacity.
